The 24-Hour Starter

By Layla on March 4, 2026 in Recipes

Home/Blog/The 24-Hour Starter

A long, cold ferment is the difference between an average loaf and one with real character. Most home bakers rush this part — a quick two-hour rise at room temperature and straight into the oven. It'll still be bread, but it won't taste like much.

Our starter has been fed the same way, twice a day, for almost as long as the bakery has existed. It's a living culture of wild yeast and bacteria, and the way we treat it in the last 24 hours before baking is what separates a soft, forgettable loaf from one with a real crust, an open crumb, and that faint tang everyone associates with "proper" sourdough.

"Flavour isn't an ingredient you add — it's time you don't rush."

Here's the short version of what we do every night: mix the dough in the late afternoon, let it bulk-ferment at room temperature for about four hours, shape it, then move it straight into the walk-in fridge for a slow, cold rise that lasts until the next morning's bake.

Why the cold retard matters

Cold slows the yeast down but barely touches the bacteria that produce lactic and acetic acid — the compounds behind sourdough's signature tang. Stretch that stage out overnight and you get noticeably more flavour without any extra proofing risk, because a cold dough is far more forgiving to shape and score.

By the time these loaves come out of the fridge in the morning, they've had somewhere between fourteen and eighteen hours of slow fermentation — long enough to build real depth, short enough that the dough hasn't over-proofed and gone slack.

If you're trying this at home, the biggest mistake we see is skipping the cold stage entirely because it "takes too long." Give it the overnight rest. Your oven spring, your crust colour, and your crumb will all thank you for it.
